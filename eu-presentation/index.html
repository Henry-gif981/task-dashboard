Introduction

This report addresses the topic: describe your understanding of the European Union (EU) and analyze the role and influence of the EU on the integration and linkage of the region, the world and Vietnam.

The European Union represents one of the most advanced examples of regional economic integration. What began primarily as an initiative to promote economic cooperation among European countries gradually developed into a highly institutionalized system involving a single market, common policies, a common currency for many members, and supranational institutions.

Chapter 1 presents the theoretical basis of regional economic integration and an overview of the EU. Chapter 2 analyzes the EU's role and influence on regional and global integration. Chapter 3 examines the EU's influence on Vietnam, with the EVFTA and EVIPA as key channels of influence.

1.1.1

Concept of international/regional economic integration

  • International economic integration refers to the process through which countries increasingly reduce barriers to the movement of goods, services, capital, people, and, in some cases, economic policies across national borders.
  • Regional economic integration: neighboring or geographically connected countries remove economic barriers and coordinate their policies in order to create a more integrated regional market.
  • The primary objective is to increase economic efficiency, expand market access, promote trade and investment, and enhance the competitiveness of participating economies.
  • Regional integration can also contribute to broader political objectives, such as maintaining peace, strengthening political stability, and increasing the collective bargaining power of member countries.

Costs and challenges

Greater market openness may expose domestic firms to stronger competition from foreign enterprises. Workers may face adjustment costs as production shifts between countries, and deeper integration may require governments to transfer certain policy responsibilities to regional institutions.

1.1

Foundational theories of economic integration

Viner (1950)

Trade creation and trade diversion

Trade creation occurs when a preferential agreement shifts purchases from higher-cost domestic production to lower-cost producers in a partner country. Trade diversion occurs when imports shift from a lower-cost non-member to a higher-cost member because of the preferential tariff. The welfare effect of an integration agreement depends on which effect dominates.

Balassa (1961)

Stages of economic integration

Integration is described as a sequence of stages of increasing depth: free trade area, customs union, common market, economic union and complete economic integration. Each stage removes further barriers and requires more policy coordination among members.

Source: Viner (1950); Balassa (1961).

1.1.2

Levels of regional economic integration

Political union can be considered an even deeper form of integration, although it is rarely achieved in practice. These stages should not necessarily be understood as a rigid sequence that every regional organization must follow.

1

Free Trade Area

Members eliminate or reduce tariffs on goods traded among themselves; each member retains its own trade policy toward non-member countries.

2

Customs Union

Establishes a common external tariff toward non-member countries.

3

Common Market

Restrictions on the movement of capital, labor, and services are substantially reduced.

4

Economic Union

Members may coordinate policies concerning taxation, competition, government spending and employment.

5

Economic and Monetary Union

Members may adopt a common currency managed by a common central bank. The euro area is the most prominent contemporary example.

1.1.3

The EU as a Model of Deep Regional Integration

The EU should not be understood simply as a trade bloc. It represents a multidimensional form of regional integration combining economic integration, legal cooperation, institutional governance, political coordination, and external relations.

Single European Market

Based on the free movement of goods, services, capital, and people.

Common policies

Trade, agriculture, competition, regional development, environmental protection, and consumer protection.

Economic and Monetary Union

Many member states have adopted the euro; monetary policy for the euro area is conducted by the European Central Bank.

Supranational institutional structure

The European Commission, the European Parliament, and the Court of Justice of the European Union exercise powers at the EU level.

External influence

EU rules and standards can influence companies and countries outside its borders, the "Brussels Effect".

1.2.2

Institutional structure

General interests of the EU

European Commission

Proposes legislation, implements EU policies, manages the EU budget, and ensures that EU law is properly applied.

EU citizens

European Parliament

Elected by citizens; participates in the legislative process, approves the budget together with the Council, and exercises democratic oversight.

Governments of the member states

Council of the European Union

National ministers meet depending on the policy area; adopts EU legislation together with the European Parliament.

Heads of state or government

European Council

Defines the Union's overall political direction and priorities; generally does not adopt ordinary legislation.

EU law

Court of Justice of the European Union

Ensures that EU law is interpreted and applied consistently across member states.

Euro area

European Central Bank

Responsible for monetary policy in the euro area; its primary objective is to maintain price stability.

EU decision-making involves a complex interaction between supranational and intergovernmental elements.

1.2.3

Objectives, Principles and Fundamental Values

Objective

Economic

An internal market in which economic barriers between member states are minimized; sustainable economic growth, employment, competitiveness, social and territorial cohesion.

Objective

Political

Maintaining peace and stability; cooperation in foreign policy, security, justice, and other areas.

Values

Fundamental values

Respect for human dignity, freedom, democracy, equality, the rule of law, and human rights.

Principle

Subsidiarity

Decisions are taken at the EU level only when Union action can achieve objectives more effectively than action at the national, regional, or local level.

Principle

Proportionality

EU action should not go beyond what is necessary to achieve the objectives established by the treaties.

Principle

Solidarity

Financial and regional policies are designed to reduce disparities and support less-developed regions.

1.2.4

Core Mechanisms of European Integration

The European Single Market

  • Free movement of goods, services, capital, and people
  • Common product standards reduce transaction costs

Common Policies

  • Common Agricultural Policy (CAP)
  • Competition policy: cartels, abuse of dominant positions, state aid
  • Regional and cohesion policies; environmental and climate policies

Economic and Monetary Union

  • The euro eliminates exchange-rate uncertainty and increases price transparency
  • Euro-area countries cannot independently conduct national monetary policy

Common Trade Policy

  • The EU negotiates many trade agreements collectively
  • The size of the integrated market strengthens its bargaining position

Supranational Governance

  • EU-level rules can be legally binding within member states
  • A political debate concerning national sovereignty

2.1.1

Deepening integration

  • Using the stages set out by Balassa (1961): a customs union (completed in 1968), a single market (in place from 1993), and an economic and monetary union.
  • The Schengen area removed most internal border checks; the Treaty of Lisbon (in force since 2009) gave the Union a single legal personality, a permanent President of the European Council and a High Representative for foreign policy.
  • "Failing forward" (Jones et al., 2016): the euro crisis of 2010–2012 produced the European Stability Mechanism and the banking union; the COVID-19 shock produced NextGenerationEU, financed by joint EU borrowing.
  • Cohesion policy transfers funds to poorer regions, and it has helped lower-income members catch up.

member states use the euro as official currency

EUR 800 bn+

NextGenerationEU in current prices (EUR 750 billion in 2018 prices)

Source: Balassa (1961); European Union (n.d.-a); Jones et al. (2016); European Commission (n.d.-h).

2.1.2a

Enlargement and Neighborhood Policy

  • Enlargement is the EU's most effective foreign policy tool, in the sense that candidates change their own laws and institutions in order to join.
  • The Copenhagen criteria of 1993 require stable democratic institutions, a functioning market economy and the capacity to take on the EU's body of law.
  • Schimmelfennig and Sedelmeier (2004): "governance by conditionality"; the prospect of membership is the reward, and rule adoption is the price.
  • The 2004 enlargement, which brought in ten countries, mostly from Central and Eastern Europe, is the clearest example.

2.1.2c

Enlargement and Neighborhood Policy

The current round

Shaped by security more than by economics

  • Ukraine and Moldova applied after Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022 and received candidate status in June 2022
  • The Commission's 2025 Enlargement Package, adopted on 4 November 2025, describes enlargement as a priority of the Union
  • Progress depends on reform in the candidates and on unanimity among existing members
  • Georgia's candidate status, granted in 2023: the incentive works only when the government wants to meet the conditions

Lavenex (2004): "external governance"

European Neighbourhood Policy

  • Launched in 2004, with the Eastern Partnership (2009) and the Union for the Mediterranean (2008) as regional tracks
  • The EU extends parts of its single-market rules to its neighbours without offering them membership
  • Mixed results: Moldova and Ukraine moved closer to the Union, while others in the southern neighbourhood changed little
  • In 2022 the EU also created the European Political Community

Source: European Commission (n.d.-a); Lavenex (2004).

2.1.3

Challenges to integration

1

Euro crisis

A common currency without a common fiscal policy leaves weaker economies exposed; a lasting north–south divide over solidarity.

2

Migration 2015–2016

Member states split over burden sharing; pressure on Schengen.

3

Brexit

The UK referendum of 2016 and its departure on 31 January 2020 showed that membership is reversible.

4

Rule of law

Disputes in Hungary and Poland led to Article 7 procedures and a mechanism that links EU funds to respect for the rule of law.

Competitiveness

The 2022 energy crisis and the widening gap with the United States and China. Draghi (2024) estimates that the EU needs to invest EUR 750–800 billion a year, equal to 4.4–4.7% of its GDP in 2023; Letta (2024) argues for completing the single market in services, energy and capital.

Source: Draghi (2024); Letta (2024).

2.2.2

Regulatory Power

Bradford (2020) calls this the "Brussels Effect": firms that want to sell in the EU must meet its standards, and many of them apply the same standards everywhere.

De facto effect

Firms apply EU standards everywhere

It is cheaper than running separate production lines.

→

De jure effect

Governments copy the rules

So that their exporters meet EU requirements at home.

→

Five conditions (Bradford, 2020)

When the effect is strongest

A large market, strong regulatory capacity, a preference for strict rules, targets that cannot easily leave the market, and products that are hard to divide by jurisdiction.

GDPR

In application since May 2018; a reference point for privacy laws in many countries.

REACH and product standards

Rules on chemicals, food safety and product standards work in the same way.

Digital and AI regulation

Digital Markets Act, Digital Services Act and AI Act, which entered into force in 2024.

CBAM

Definitive regime from 1 January 2026: importers of cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen must buy certificates.

Two sides

For the EU it is a low-cost way to spread its values. For exporters in developing countries it can be a burden, since compliance requires technology, testing and finance that small firms often lack.

On 19 November 2025 the Commission proposed a Digital Omnibus that would delay the AI Act's rules for high-risk systems until December 2027 and simplify parts of the GDPR (Akin Gump, 2025).

Source: Bradford (2020); European Commission (n.d.-e); Akin Gump (2025).

2.3.1

Climate Leadership and Sustainability

55%

reduction of net greenhouse-gas emissions by at least 55% by 2030 compared with 1990 (European Climate Law)

90%

net reduction by 2040: 85% domestic and up to 5% international carbon credits (March 2026)

  • The EU launched the first large carbon market, the Emissions Trading System, in 2005, and played a central role in bringing about the Paris Agreement of 2015.
  • Influence works through example and negotiation, market power (the Emissions Trading System and CBAM), and finance and standards.

Limits

The EU's share of global greenhouse-gas emissions fell from 6.1% in 2023 to 5.9% in 2024, and its emissions were about 35% below 1990 levels, so its goals cannot be reached without larger emitters.

2.3.2

Security and External Action

Bull (1982); Manners (2002)

"Civilian power" and "normative power"

  • The Common Foreign and Security Policy is decided mostly by unanimity
  • The High Representative leads the European External Action Service
  • CSDP: 22 missions and operations: 13 civilian, 8 military, 1 civilian-military; more than 40 launched since 2003

Since 2022

Sanctions and defence

  • By July 2026 the Council had adopted 21 packages against Russia, the latest on 23 July 2026
  • About EUR 210 billion in Russian central bank assets immobilised in the EU
  • European Peace Facility; Strategic Compass (2022); SAFE loan instrument of EUR 150 billion, adopted on 27 May 2025

Limits

Not a military power

  • NATO remains the core of collective defence for most members
  • Defence budgets and procurement remain national
  • Unanimity can slow decisions
  • "Civilian power" or "soft imperialism" (Hettne and Söderbaum, 2005)

Source: Bull (1982); Manners (2002); Council of the European Union (n.d.-a, n.d.-b, 2025); Hettne and Söderbaum (2005).

2.3.3

The EU as a Model for Other Regional Organizations

  • Börzel and Risse (2012) separate three mechanisms of diffusion: active promotion through funding and agreements, learning, and borrowing institutional forms because they carry legitimacy.
  • The African Union (2002), whose structure includes a commission, an assembly and a court, borrowed visibly from European forms.
  • Mercosur (1991) set a customs union as a goal; its Parliament and dispute-settlement mechanisms follow European ideas.
  • The EU prefers to sign agreements with regional blocs where it can, described by Hettne and Söderbaum (2005) as "interregionalism".
  • ASEAN built an Economic Community in 2015 on a different philosophy: consensus, non-interference and no supranational court.

The EU exports ideas and practices more successfully than its institutional design (Börzel and Risse, 2012).

Source: Börzel and Risse (2012); Hettne and Söderbaum (2005).

2.4.2

Future Prospects

1

Institutional

Welcoming Ukraine, Moldova and the Western Balkans would require rethinking decision rules, the budget and agricultural and cohesion policies.

2

Competitiveness

Following Draghi (2024) and Letta (2024): reduce fragmentation of the single market and raise investment in technology, energy and defence.

3

Strategic autonomy

Trade diversification through agreements with Mercosur, India and Southeast Asian partners; measures on supply chains and critical raw materials.

4

Green ambition and cost

The 2040 climate target and CBAM define the European line; the political space to maintain them depends on whether the transition raises growth.

For the rest of the world, including Vietnam, the EU will probably remain a major source of market access, capital and rules, but it will ask more in return: stricter environmental and labour standards, supply-chain due diligence and political alignment on some strategic issues.

Source: Draghi (2024); Letta (2024).

3.2.1

Content and timeline

In Opinion 2/15 (May 2017), the Court of Justice of the EU ruled that investment protection with arbitration-style dispute settlement falls under shared competence. In response, the EU split the package into two instruments.

Trade; EU-only agreement

EVFTA: a "new-generation" agreement

  • Trade in goods: tariff elimination, rules of origin, customs and trade facilitation, trade remedies, TBT and SPS measures
  • Services and investment liberalisation
  • Government procurement, competition policy, state-owned enterprises and intellectual property, including geographical indications
  • Trade and Sustainable Development: core ILO conventions, environmental protection and climate action
  • Institutional provisions and dispute settlement

Investment protection; requires ratification by every member state

EVIPA

  • Fair and equitable treatment and protection against expropriation
  • Four chapters, 92 articles and 13 annexes; designed to replace 21 existing bilateral investment agreements
  • A court-style dispute settlement mechanism replaces traditional investor–state arbitration
  • Poland became the 20th member state to ratify it in November 2025; the EVIPA is not yet in force

3.3

EU's Broader Influence on Vietnam's Development

European standards and regulatory requirements increasingly affect how Vietnamese businesses operate and access international markets. Three requirements discussed in the report are CBAM, EUDR and IUU.

CBAM

Carbon content of imports

The definitive regime applies from 1 January 2026, after a transitional phase from 2023 to 2025: importers of cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen must buy certificates reflecting the carbon content of their goods (European Commission, n.d.-e). A national system for measuring, reporting and verifying emissions would let Vietnamese firms supply credible data to EU importers (East Asia Forum, 2026; S&P Global, 2025).

3.4.1

Opportunities

  1. Greater access to the European market and export diversification: lower tariffs and clearer market-access conditions, particularly for textiles and garments, footwear, electronics, furniture, agricultural products and seafood.
  2. Higher-quality investment and technology: advanced manufacturing, renewable energy, logistics, digital infrastructure, semiconductors, sustainable agriculture and the circular economy.
  3. Stronger support for Vietnam's green transition: through the Just Energy Transition Partnership and Global Gateway.
  4. Improvements in institutional and regulatory quality: transparency, regulatory predictability, market access and labour and environmental standards.
  5. Human-capital and technological development: demand for skilled workers in advanced manufacturing, digital technology, renewable energy, environmental management, logistics and sustainable finance.

3.4.2

Challenges

  1. Increasing compliance costs for Vietnamese enterprises: small and medium-sized enterprises often have limited financial resources, technological capacity and access to compliance services.
  2. Limited domestic capacity to capture high-value investment and technology: weak supporting industries, limited research and development capacity and shortages of highly skilled labour.
  3. Increasing pressure for institutional and regulatory adjustment: European regulations on climate, carbon emissions, digitalisation and supply-chain responsibility are continuously evolving.
  4. Financing and infrastructure constraints for the green transition: renewable energy, electricity grids, energy storage, sustainable transport and climate-resilient infrastructure.
  5. Shortages of skilled human resources and innovation capacity: gaps between the skills demanded by businesses and those provided by education and vocational training.

3.4.3

Recommendations

1

Complete the EVIPA and manage the surplus diplomatically

Continue parliamentary and diplomatic engagement with the remaining member states.

2

Operationalise carbon pricing and emissions data

Launch the pilot emissions trading exchange on schedule and build a national measuring, reporting and verification system.

3

Build traceability systems for EUDR and IUU compliance

Digital forest-plot mapping and geolocation for coffee, rubber and timber; fleet monitoring and enforcement.

4

Raise EVFTA utilisation

Simplify customs and certificate-of-origin procedures, speed up tax refunds, digitalise documentation and apply rules of origin uniformly.

5

Make JETP and Global Gateway funds bankable

A pipeline of prepared grid, storage and transport projects, with clear regulation and coordination among ministries.

6

Attract higher-quality EU investment and stay predictable

Link incentives to technology transfer, R&D and supplier development.

7

For firms: invest in compliance and diversification

Map supply chains, measure product-level emissions, obtain certifications and gradually move into higher-value segments.
