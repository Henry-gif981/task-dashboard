1.1.1 · Concept

Regional economic integration

  • Definition: countries progressively reduce barriers to goods, services, capital and people — and increasingly coordinate policies.
  • Two levels: global (multilateral rules for trade and investment) and regional (neighbouring countries deepen ties).
  • Economic aims: efficiency, larger markets, economies of scale, trade and investment, competitiveness.
  • Political aims: peace, stability and stronger collective bargaining power.

The costs of integration

Stronger competition for domestic firms · adjustment costs for workers as production shifts · transfer of some policy responsibilities to regional institutions.
1.1.2 · Levels

Five steps up the integration ladder

Political union is deeper still but rarely achieved. The stages are not a rigid sequence — arrangements can mix features of different stages.

1

Free Trade Area

Internal tariffs removed; each member keeps its own external trade policy (e.g. USMCA, AFTA)

2

Customs Union

+ Common external tariff toward non-members

3

Common Market

+ Free movement of capital, labour and services

4

Economic Union

+ Coordinated tax, competition, spending and employment policies

5

Economic & Monetary Union

+ Common currency and common central bank — the euro area and the ECB

1.1.3 · The EU as a model

A model of deep regional integration

The EU is not simply a trade bloc: it combines economic integration, legal cooperation, institutional governance, political coordination and external relations.

2.1.1 · Deepening integration

Further than any other regional group

  • Along Balassa's (1961) ladder: customs union 1968, single market 1993, then economic and monetary union.
  • Schengen removed most internal border checks; Lisbon (2009) gave a single legal personality, a permanent European Council President and a High Representative.
  • "Failing forward" (Jones et al., 2016): euro crisis 2010–12 → ESM and banking union; COVID-19 → NextGenerationEU, financed by joint borrowing.
  • Social side: cohesion policy helps lower-income members catch up.

2.1.2 · Enlargement

Governance by conditionality

  • Enlargement is the EU's most effective foreign-policy tool: candidates change their own laws to join.
  • Copenhagen criteria (1993): stable democracy, a functioning market economy, capacity to adopt EU law.
  • Schimmelfennig & Sedelmeier (2004): membership is the reward, rule adoption the price.
  • 2004: ten countries, mostly Central and Eastern Europe — the clearest example.

2.1.2 · Neighbourhood

Today's enlargement and the neighbourhood

Shaped by security

The current enlargement round

  • Ukraine and Moldova applied after Russia's 2022 invasion; candidates since June 2022
  • 2025 Enlargement Package (4 Nov 2025): enlargement is a priority of the Union
  • Still depends on reform and unanimity; some Western Balkan applicants have waited a decade or more
  • Georgia (candidate 2023): conditionality works only if the government wants to comply
"External governance" (Lavenex, 2004)

European Neighbourhood Policy

  • Launched 2004; Union for the Mediterranean (2008), Eastern Partnership (2009)
  • Single-market rules extended without membership
  • Mixed results: Moldova and Ukraine moved closer; the southern neighbourhood changed little
  • European Political Community (2022): influence wider than legal borders

Challenges to integration

1

Euro crisis

A common currency without a common fiscal policy; a lasting north–south divide

2

Migration 2015–16

Split over burden sharing; pressure on Schengen

3

2016 referendum, exit on 31 January 2020: membership is reversible

4

Rule of law

Hungary and Poland; Article 7 procedures; EU funds linked to the rule of law

The economic dimension: competitiveness

The 2022 energy crisis and a widening productivity gap with the US and China. Draghi (2024): investment of €750–800 bn a year (4.4–4.7% of 2023 GDP). Letta (2024): complete the single market in services, energy and capital.

2.2.2 · Regulatory power

The "Brussels Effect"

Bradford (2020): firms that want to sell in the EU must meet its standards — and many apply them everywhere.

Step 1

The EU sets strict rules

Access to a large single market requires compliance

→
Step 2 · de facto

Firms go global with them

One EU-compliant production line is cheaper than several

→
Step 3 · de jure

Governments copy the rules

So that their exporters meet EU requirements at home

Strongest when five conditions hold
1Large market2Strong regulatory capacity3Preference for strict rules4Targets that cannot easily leave5Products hard to divide by jurisdiction

GDPR (2018)

A reference point for privacy laws; global tech firms adjusted their data policies

REACH & product rules

Chemicals, food safety and product standards

Digital & AI (2024)

Digital Markets Act, Digital Services Act and AI Act

CBAM (definitive 1 Jan 2026)

Certificates for carbon in cement, iron & steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity, hydrogen

Two sides

For the EU: a low-cost way to spread values and protect firms from lower-standard competition. For developing-country exporters: a burden — technology, testing and finance that small firms often lack.
Digital Omnibus (19 Nov 2025): proposal to delay the AI Act's high-risk rules to December 2027 and simplify parts of the GDPR — still to be agreed.

Source: Bradford (2020); European Commission (n.d.-e); Akin Gump (2025).

2.3.2 · Security and external action

From civilian power to a strategic actor

Bull (1982) · Manners (2002)

"Civilian" / "normative" power

  • Common Foreign and Security Policy decided mostly by unanimity
  • The High Representative leads the EEAS
  • CSDP: 22 missions and operations today (13 civilian, 8 military); 40+ launched since 2003
Since Russia's 2022 invasion

Hard-security turn

  • 21 sanctions packages by 23 July 2026: oil, coal and LNG bans; financial restrictions
  • ~€210 bn of Russian central bank assets immobilised
  • European Peace Facility; Strategic Compass (2022); SAFE loans of €150 bn (27 May 2025)
Not a military power

Limits

  • NATO remains the core of collective defence
  • Defence budgets and procurement remain national
  • Unanimity slows decisions
  • "Civilian power" or "soft imperialism"? (Hettne & Söderbaum, 2005)

Source: Council of the European Union (n.d.-a, n.d.-b, 2025).

2.3.3 · A model for other regions

A template others borrow from

  • Three diffusion mechanisms (Börzel & Risse, 2012): active promotion, learning, borrowing for legitimacy.
  • African Union (2002): commission, assembly and court borrowed from European forms.
  • Mercosur (1991): customs-union goal; parliament and dispute settlement follow European ideas.
  • "Interregionalism" (Hettne & Söderbaum, 2005): the EU prefers agreements with regional blocs.
  • ASEAN: Economic Community (2015) built on consensus and non-interference, with no supranational court; EU–ASEAN strategic partnership since December 2020.
Lesson: the EU exports ideas and practices more successfully than its institutional design, which reflects post-war European conditions.

2.4.2 · Future prospects

Four developments that will shape the EU

1

Institutions

Admitting Ukraine, Moldova and the Western Balkans means rethinking decision rules, the budget, agriculture and cohesion

2

Competitiveness

Draghi and Letta: less single-market fragmentation, more investment in technology, energy and defence — without lowering standards

3

Strategic autonomy

Deals with Mercosur, India and Southeast Asia; supply-chain and critical raw materials measures

4

Green ambition vs cost

The 2040 target and CBAM depend on the transition raising growth, not only costs

What it means for Vietnam and the world

The EU will remain a major source of market access, capital and rules — but will ask more in return: stricter environmental and labour standards, supply-chain due diligence and political alignment on some strategic issues.

3.4.1 – 3.4.2 · Opportunities and challenges

What the EU means for Vietnam

3.4.1

Opportunities

  • Greater access to the European market and export diversification
  • Higher-quality investment and technology transfer
  • Finance and know-how for the green transition (JETP, Global Gateway)
  • Improvements in institutional and regulatory quality
  • Human-capital and technological development
3.4.2

Challenges

  • Rising compliance costs, especially for SMEs
  • Limited domestic capacity to capture high-value investment and technology
  • Constant pressure for institutional and regulatory adjustment
  • Financing and infrastructure constraints for the green transition
  • Shortages of skilled human resources and innovation capacity

3

Traceability

Geolocation for coffee, rubber and timber (EUDR); fleet monitoring and enforcement (IUU)

4

Raise EVFTA utilisation

Simpler customs and origin procedures, faster tax refunds, digital documents; help SMEs become suppliers

5

Bankable green projects

A pipeline of grid, storage and transport projects for JETP and Global Gateway funds

6

Higher-quality EU FDI

Tie incentives to technology transfer, R&D and supplier development; stay predictable

7

For firms

Map supply chains, measure product emissions, get certified and move into higher-value segments

Conclusion

Key takeaways

1

The deepest model of regional integration

Single market, euro and supranational institutions — built through both deepening and widening since 1950.

2

Influence built on market power and rules

40+ trade agreements, the Brussels Effect, CBAM, binding climate targets and the world's largest aid effort.

3

Strengths are structural, limits are political

Unanimity, Euroscepticism, reliance on NATO and the compliance burden abroad constrain its role.

4

For Vietnam: a partner for growth and standards

EVFTA-era trade rose from US$49.8 bn (2019) to US$68.3 bn (2024); next come standards, the green transition and EVIPA ratification.
