Introduction

Regional economic integration is one of the defining features of the world economy. The European Union is the most advanced and most institutionalised model: it began in 1951 as a six-country community for coal and steel and has grown into a union of 27 member states with a single market, a common trade policy, a common currency for most members, and supranational institutions whose decisions bind national governments.

For Vietnam, the EU is a major export market, a source of investment and technology, and an increasingly demanding standard-setter, especially since the EVFTA and the EVIPA. The report describes the EU as a model of regional integration and analyses its role and influence on the region, the world and Vietnam, using integration theory, EU policy documents, academic literature, and trade and investment data.

Chapter 1 presents the theoretical basis of regional economic integration and an overview of the EU. Chapter 2 analyses the EU's role in European integration, global economic integration and global governance. Chapter 3 examines the EU's influence on Vietnam through the EVFTA and EVIPA, standards and the green transition, and sets out opportunities, challenges and policy recommendations.

1.2.3

Objectives, Principles and Core Policies

The objectives define the goals of European integration, the principles provide its legal and political foundation, and the core policies translate these commitments into practice.

Article 3 TEU

Objectives

  • Peace, fundamental values and citizens' well-being
  • An area of freedom, security and justice
  • The internal market: goods, services, capital and people
  • Sustainable economic and social development: balanced growth, price stability, employment, environmental protection
  • Economic, social and territorial cohesion
  • A stronger global role: peace, security, sustainable development, free and fair trade

EU treaties

Principles

  • Human dignity, freedom, democracy, equality, the rule of law and human rights (Article 2 TEU)
  • Conferral: only the powers granted by the treaties
  • Subsidiarity: EU action only where member states cannot sufficiently achieve the objectives
  • Proportionality: no more than what is necessary
  • Solidarity among member states
  • Sincere cooperation between the EU and its member states

Common policies

Core policies

  • Single Market and Customs Union (common external tariff)
  • Economic and Monetary Policy (ECB, euro area)
  • Common Commercial Policy
  • Common Agricultural Policy (CAP)
  • Cohesion Policy
  • Environmental and Climate Policy (European Green Deal)
  • Energy and Transport Policy
  • Competition and Consumer Protection Policy
  • Digital and Innovation Policy
  • Foreign and Security Policy

Together, they contribute to regional stability, economic integration, sustainable development and the EU's influence in global affairs.

Source: Treaty on European Union (Articles 2 and 3).

2.1.1a

Economic Integration

Economic integration has been the engine of the European project, moving from a common market for coal and steel to a single market for the whole economy.

1951 · 1957

Treaties of Paris and Rome

A common market for coal and steel, then a customs union and the gradual creation of a common market among the six founding members. Internal tariffs were abolished by 1968; a common external tariff, the CAP and a common commercial policy followed. The main effect was trade creation among the members.

1986 · end of 1992

Single European Act

The programme to complete the internal market relied on mutual recognition, minimum harmonisation and the removal of nearly 300 legislative and administrative obstacles.

Guaranteed by the treaties

The four freedoms

Free movement of goods, services, capital and persons, enforced by the European Commission and the Court of Justice of the European Union.

Monetary integration

The Maastricht Treaty set a three-stage path to EMU with convergence criteria. The euro was launched as an accounting currency in 1999 in 11 Member States and as cash in 2002. Only six Member States remain outside: Czechia, Denmark, Hungary, Poland, Romania and Sweden. The euro is the second most important international currency, with around one fifth of global official foreign-exchange reserves.

From "negative" to "positive" integration

Beyond removing barriers, the EU redistributes and coordinates: cohesion policy, the CAP, competition and State-aid control, and common rules for transport, energy networks, consumer protection and the digital economy.

2.1.2a

Political Cooperation and Regional Stability

  • The Schuman Declaration of 9 May 1950 aimed to make war between European states "not merely unthinkable but materially impossible"; the EU's contribution to peace was recognised by the Nobel Peace Prize in 2012.
  • Enlargement is the EU's most powerful instrument for projecting stability: it helped consolidate democracy in Greece, Portugal and Spain, and drove reform in Central and Eastern Europe after 1989.
  • The Copenhagen criteria (1993): stable democratic institutions, the rule of law, human rights and respect for minorities; a functioning market economy; the ability to take on the acquis.
  • Ten countries joined in 2004, Bulgaria and Romania in 2007 and Croatia in 2013, raising membership to 28 before the United Kingdom left in 2020.

Beyond enlargement

Neighbourhood and CFSP/CSDP

  • European Neighbourhood Policy, association and partnership agreements, conditional financial support
  • CFSP and CSDP: civilian and military missions, sanctions, coordinated diplomacy
  • Member States remain the main holders of military capability; NATO remains the primary framework for collective defence

Since 2022

A growing security dimension

  • Successive packages of sanctions against Russia
  • Financial, humanitarian and military assistance to Ukraine, partly through the European Peace Facility
  • "ReArm Europe / Readiness 2030" (2025): aims to mobilise up to EUR 800 billion over the coming years

  • Brexit showed that integration is not irreversible, and the cost of leaving the single market
  • Rule-of-law concerns: infringement procedures, the Article 7 procedure and budget conditionality

Seen as a whole, the EU's record in promoting regional stability is strong: a zone of peace and prosperity that has proved attractive to its neighbours and resilient to repeated crises.

2.2.1b

Global Trade and Investment

Vulnerabilities

The energy deficit widened from EUR 226 billion in 2015 to EUR 299 billion in 2025, and "other manufactured goods" moved from a surplus of EUR 11.3 billion to a deficit of EUR 37.6 billion, reflecting increased import competition, particularly from China (Figure 2.4).

Concentration

The five largest non-EU partners account for more than half of goods trade and more than three fifths of services trade, one reason why diversification of partners has become a priority in EU trade policy.

More than half (54.4%) of extra-EU goods trade consists of intermediate goods: the EU is a hub of cross-border production networks.

2.2.2b

International Cooperation and Global Governance

≈40%

of global official development assistance comes from the Union institutions and the Member States together, making the EU the largest provider of development cooperation and one of the leading humanitarian donors

Up to EUR 300 billion

to be mobilised by 2027 under the Global Gateway strategy (launched December 2021) for infrastructure, digital, energy, transport, health and education

Peace and security

Missions, sanctions, Ukraine

  • Civilian missions and military operations in the Balkans, Africa, the Middle East and elsewhere
  • Unprecedented sanctions on Russia; support through the European Peace Facility; temporary protection for millions of Ukrainian refugees; a path to Ukraine's accession
  • Exposed military dependence on the United States and a debate on "strategic autonomy"

Global governance and Asia

Law, coalitions, partnerships

  • Support for the International Criminal Court, human rights mechanisms and arms control
  • Indo-Pacific strategy (2021): trade, sustainability and the rule of law
  • Vietnam: partnership and cooperation agreement, trade agreement and JETP climate finance

Limits

Less weight than its economy

  • No seat in the IMF or the UN Security Council, where only France is a permanent member
  • Unanimity in foreign and security policy
  • National interests, different strategic cultures and a limited common budget
  • A less favourable environment: trade, technology and finance used as geopolitical instruments

The Union's influence is therefore strongest where it can act through law, regulation and markets. The effectiveness of Global Gateway depends on attracting private capital and coordinating Member States' financial institutions.

  • The EU and its Member States were among the strongest advocates of the 2030 Agenda and the 2015 Paris Agreement. The ETS (2005) is the oldest and one of the largest carbon markets.

A turning point: the EVFTA

Signed in 2019 and in force since 1 August 2020, it cut tariffs and opened markets for goods and services, and added commitments on labour rights, environmental protection, intellectual property, government procurement and sustainable development, so its impact goes beyond lower trade barriers.

Beyond trade

Climate change, the energy transition, green growth and sustainable finance have become more prominent. Through technical assistance, policy dialogue and development cooperation, the EU increasingly influences Vietnam's energy, environmental and green-investment policies.

Content

3.2.1c

The impact of the EVFTA on bilateral trade

The product structure confirms that the two economies are complementary.

  • 2025 EU imports from Vietnam: machinery and transport equipment, including electronics and phones, about 51% (€32.4 billion); miscellaneous manufactures such as footwear, clothing and furniture another 29% (€18.7 billion).
  • EU exports to Vietnam are smaller and more technology-intensive: machinery and transport equipment (36% of the total) and chemicals and pharmaceuticals (26%).
  • Vietnam's three largest export lines to the EU in 2025: computers and electronics (US$10.9 billion), machinery and equipment (US$7.4 billion), phones and components (US$6.9 billion).

3.2.1d

  • European investors are present in manufacturing, energy, infrastructure, finance, food and beverages and information technology.
  • EEAS: more than US$27.8 billion in about 2,450 projects by the end of 2022, over 60% in manufacturing; the EU is Vietnam's sixth-largest source of FDI. Vietnamese media citing official data speak of nearly 2,500 European projects in 2025.
  • The Netherlands is the largest EU investor (US$13.7 billion at end-2022), followed by France, Luxembourg, Germany and Denmark; Denmark's LEGO plant in Ho Chi Minh City is among the largest single projects.

Quality may matter more than quantity

European firms bring advanced technology, management practices and environmental standards; the agreement gives investors and suppliers a reason to meet requirements on quality, traceability, labour and environment. This supports technology transfer, productivity, jobs and training; Danish, German and other European firms have been especially active in renewable energy.

3.2.2

The EU's influence on Vietnam's economic structure and competitiveness

By attaching conditions to market access and finance, the EU affects what Vietnam produces, the standards it meets and the rules under which firms operate.

1

Export structure and position in value chains

Exports concentrated in electronics, machinery, garments, footwear and furniture (Figure 3.2); much growth comes from foreign-invested assembly, so value added captured in Vietnam is lower than headline figures. Rules of origin and standards push upgrading; moving up the value chain is the main long-term question.

2

Institutional and regulatory reform

Both agreements require a more transparent, predictable and rules-based environment and act as external drivers of change (Table 3.8). The Trade Committee and specialised committees give businesses a formal channel; both sides stressed their importance again in 2026.

3

Green transition

Conditions on standards and finance link market access to environmental performance (see Section 3.3).

3.3

EU's Broader Influence on Vietnam's Development

Beyond trade, investment and the reforms tied to the EVFTA and EVIPA, the EU influences Vietnam through two connected dimensions: European standards and regulatory requirements that shape how Vietnamese businesses operate, and development cooperation that finances sustainable infrastructure, renewable energy and climate projects, supporting Vietnam's commitment to net-zero emissions by 2050.

3.4.1

Opportunities

  1. Greater access to the European market and export diversification: lower tariffs and clearer conditions for textiles and garments, footwear, electronics, furniture, agricultural products and seafood, and less dependence on a few markets. Trade growth reflects the EVFTA together with global demand, supply chains and wider integration.
  2. Higher-quality investment and technology: advanced manufacturing, renewable energy, logistics, digital infrastructure, semiconductors, sustainable agriculture and the circular economy, shifting investment away from low labour costs toward higher value added.
  3. Stronger support for Vietnam's green transition: finance, technology and institutions through the JETP and Global Gateway; the Comprehensive Strategic Partnership stresses clean energy, decarbonisation, circular economy, sustainable transport and carbon pricing.
  4. Improvements in institutional and regulatory quality: transparency, predictability, market access and labour and environmental standards, supported by the EVFTA's mechanisms and EU technical support for green-trade rules.
  5. Human-capital and technological development: demand for skilled workers in advanced manufacturing, digital technology, renewable energy, environmental management, logistics and sustainable finance; cooperation in education, training, science and innovation.

3.4.2

Challenges

  1. Increasing compliance costs for Vietnamese enterprises: quality, technical standards, traceability, environment, labour and sustainability requirements raise costs; SMEs have limited finance, technology and access to compliance services.
  2. Limited domestic capacity to capture high-value investment and technology: weak supporting industries, limited R&D and shortages of highly skilled labour risk FDI without deeper domestic participation.
  3. Increasing pressure for institutional and regulatory adjustment: EU rules on climate, carbon, digitalisation and supply-chain responsibility keep evolving; new requirements without transition periods or guidance create uncertainty.
  4. Financing and infrastructure constraints for the green transition: renewable energy, grids, storage, sustainable transport and resilient infrastructure need more than external finance; project preparation, procedures, grid capacity and long payback periods slow delivery.
  5. Shortages of skilled human resources and innovation capacity: gaps between the skills businesses need and those supplied by education and vocational training limit the benefits of technology transfer.

3.4.3

Recommendations

1

Complete the EVIPA and manage the surplus diplomatically

Continue parliamentary and diplomatic engagement with the remaining member states, using the Comprehensive Strategic Partnership roadmap, while committing to market-access improvements and balanced trade in EU-origin goods, so that the surplus does not erode political support.

2

Operationalise carbon pricing and emissions data

Launch the pilot emissions trading exchange on schedule and build a national measuring, reporting and verification system, so firms can supply credible data to EU importers and keep more carbon revenue at home (East Asia Forum, 2026; S&P Global, 2025).

3

Build traceability systems for EUDR and IUU compliance

Digital forest-plot mapping and geolocation for coffee, rubber and timber, and full implementation of fleet monitoring, traceability and enforcement measures requested by the Commission (Trung tâm WTO và Hội nhập, 2026b; Vietnam News Agency, 2026).

4

Raise EVFTA utilisation

Simplify customs and certificate-of-origin procedures, speed up tax refunds, digitalise documentation and apply rules of origin uniformly, as EuroCham has recommended (Thương hiệu & Công luận, 2026); help SMEs become qualified suppliers to foreign-invested exporters to raise local content.

5

Make JETP and Global Gateway funds bankable

A pipeline of prepared grid, storage and transport projects, with clear regulation and coordination among ministries, would improve the absorption of EU concessional finance and attract co-investment (Trung tâm WTO và Hội nhập, 2026c; Climate Policy Initiative, n.d.).

6

Attract higher-quality EU investment and stay predictable

Link incentives to technology transfer, R&D and supplier development, and keep the regulatory environment stable, to reinforce the benefits of EU FDI beyond its modest share of annual inflows (Italian Trade Agency, 2026; Vietnam Investment Review, 2025).

7

For firms: invest in compliance and diversification

Map supply chains, measure product-level emissions, obtain certifications and gradually move into higher-value segments rather than relying on assembly-based exports, using industry associations for technical training.

Conclusion

1

More than a trade bloc

A multidimensional integration system: from coal and steel through the customs union, the Single Market and EMU, usually by repairing gaps exposed by crises (the euro crisis, COVID-19) and through repeated enlargements. Its influence rests on market size and a body of rules. Its strengths are structural; its limits are political (unanimity among 27 governments, enlargement fatigue, dependence on NATO, the cost of its own regulation), so it is strongest in trade and regulation and weaker in foreign and defence policy.

2

One of Vietnam's most important partners

Under the EVFTA, bilateral trade rose from about US$49.8 billion in 2019 to about US$68 billion in 2024, with a large Vietnamese surplus, while EU cooperation supported institutional reform, higher standards and the green transition (JETP, Global Gateway). The benefits are not automatic: stricter rules on sustainability, traceability and carbon pricing raise costs, especially for SMEs, and expose weaknesses in supporting industries, skills and green finance.

3

Making the most of it

Complete the EVIPA ratification and manage the surplus diplomatically, raise the use of EVFTA preferences, build traceability and emissions-measurement systems, make green finance projects bankable, and attract EU investment that transfers technology and links with domestic suppliers. Firms should invest in compliance and move into higher-value segments.

The EU experience shows that integration brings lasting benefits only when it is backed by sound institutions and policies that spread its gains and cushion its costs.
